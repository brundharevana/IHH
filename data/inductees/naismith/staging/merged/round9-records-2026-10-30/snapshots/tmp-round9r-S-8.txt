easy for Mr. Elmekki. who al
ways has more ideas than he
can use. For him the prob
lem is selection.
The space on a stamp is
small. The subject must be
simple and clear, something
that can be grasped at a
glance. So usually the artist
must resort to symbolism.
The representation should
be realistic, Mr. Elmekki be
lieves. but should always
leave something to the imagi
nation, so that each person
who sees it can complete it
from his own experience.
For an Africa Day stamp
the symbol is an obvious one
—an outline map of Africa.
But if you look closely at
Mr. Elmekki’s first prize de
sign for this year's stamps,
the map is really two clasped
hands, one white, one black.
Here is the friendly relation
ship of Arab and Negro Afri
ca. If you look closer still,
the hands are clasped not in
the conventional handshake
but in away which suggests
Indian wrestling. Is Mr. El
mekki suggesting friendly
competition rather than sim
ply friendship?
Lettering is always a prob
lem for the stamp designer,
and Mr. Elmekki has it in
double measure, since all
wording must appear in both
Arabic and. French. Often he
can work an inscription in
as a design feature. A long
inscription on a Scout stamp
becomes a flagpole. On a
Stamp Day issue the Arabic
designation suggests the
domes and minarets of an
Arab town.
The humor comes mostly
from the bizarre or unexpect
ed—for National Day an at
tractive girl in Tunisian garb
riding a crescent moon: for
Stamp Day a mailman on a
comic camel using a tele
phone to nowhere; for the
Scout jamboree a hand in
the three-finger Scout sign
but with the fingers ending
in miniature heads and the
cuff, on closer inspection, a
tent.
Mr. Elmekki’s success has
encouraged other artists to
try their own versions of the
lighthearted approach, and
most recent Tunisian stamps
show its influence.
Not quite all. however. The
most recent issue with a por
trait of President Habib
Bourguiba 'not by Mr. El
mekki) shows him in a very
dignified pose in very formal
clothes. Mr. Elmekki’s 1959
stamp recalling Mr. Bourgui
ba’s exile pictured him in
badly rumpled trousers. Mr.
Bourguiba was not amused.

NUMISMATIC NOTES
Legal Tender Cents
One cent pieces have not
always been legal tender in
the United States.
When the coinage law of
February 21, 1857, which pro
vided for small cents, was
approved, its main purpose
was to eliminate from circu
lation Spanish and Mexican
coins then widely used. It
made no provision for re
demption of the cents.
Small coins of all kinds
were scarce during the Civil
War and it was not until well
after the fighting ended that
the unredeemable cents be
gan to become a nuisance,
particularly to postmasters
and business firms.

of Holley Sheldon of Hud
son. N. Y.. wrote to the Sec
retary of the Treasury:

we can dispose of pennies?
The country is flooded with
all kinds, large and small,

to complement existing light

from surfaces other than
white or gray when working
in color.

his favorite chair, or a teen
age girl at her dressing table

these familiar settings. A re
laxed. comfortable subject is
an important requirement for
a successful portrait session.

Verdi z s Otello Heard
In Two New Versions
By HERMAN SCHADEN
Btar Record Critic
Giuseppe Verdi’s Otello
currently is playing an oper
atic doubleheader in the re
cord marts.
Collectors in quest of a
new stereo or mono version
of the epic tragedy have a
choice between productions
by London and RCA Victor.
London s lineup directed by
Herbert von Karajan has the
well-known names of Mario
del Monaco as Otello and
Renata Tebaldi as Desde
mona, with Aldo Protti as
lago. The choral work is by
the Vienna State Opera and
the orchestra is the Vienna
Philharmonic, one of Von
Karajan’s many interests.
Tullio Serafin Conducts
RCA Victori’s version is
done in its Soria Series, fin
ery. which means some dandy
frills in packaging and beau
tifully illustrated notes and
libretto. Its leading singers
are Jon Vickers (Otello), Leo
nie Rysanek (Desdemona)
I and Tito Gobbi (Iago). The
venerable and redoubtable
Tullio Serafin is at the helm
of the Rome Opera Orchestra
and Chorus.
Either set will bring some
moments of pleasure if not
the so-called definitive per
formance. Each set has its
merits and debits. In Del
Monaco and Tebaldi, London
has two singers who know
their parts well, and inject
into them the fire and pas
sion they must have. This
particularly is true of Del
Monaco, whose dark por
trayal offsets occasional vocal
harshness.
A Fresh Tenor Voice
On the other hand. Victor
presents the relatively new
name of Vickers in the title
role. His is a voice of lyric
splendor, but his' dramatic
effort is short of the mark.
So is Miss Rysanek’s voice
pretty to hear, lighter than
Telbaldi’s, but again she is
not so involved in the plot.
The Victor set is fortunate
in having as its lago Tito
Gobbi, a remarkably fine art
ist and sonorous baritone. His
opposite for London, Protti,
is not so strong.
Von Karajan is a solid con
ductor, getting big dramatic
effects from the principals,
chorus and orchestra. The
London version has a darker
cast than that of the Victor
set. Serafin perhaps evokes
a more Italian flavor, and
builds nicely to climaxes, but
at times moves too slowly.
His quieter scenes, like the
love duet and Desdemona's
bedroom scene, elicit intense
beauty.
