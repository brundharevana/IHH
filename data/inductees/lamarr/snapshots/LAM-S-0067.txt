By JUNE GREENE
NEA Service Special Correspondent
Pittsburgh, Aug. 30—Dancing goes patriotic.
Not the dawnse as practiced by metropolitan esthetes, but the
dance—the common garden variety routines you’ll be seeing next winter
at college proms, in night clubs, ballrooms and Juke Joints.
Young America may not know it yet, but its favorite terpsichorean
traipsings in the near future are going to have a distinctly American
flavor.
Take the titles, for instance. "Liberty” and “Plymouth Rock” are
the two routines, say the Dancing Masters of America, which are des
tined to be the most popular in the coming year.
"Liberty Parade,” which got the teachers’ official sanction at their
recent 17-day convention held in Pittsburgh, is danced to “God Bless
America,” “Yankee Doodle,” “Dixie” and “California, Here I come,” in
that order.
The convention sounded the death knell for the London-inspired
"Lambeth Walk” and the “Boomps-a-Daisy,” both of which were in
vogue during the last two years.
Many factors influenced composition of “Liberty Parade,” says Leroy
Thayer of Washington, D. C., the dancing masters’ president.
“With a war in Europe, the draft controversy and national defense
major issues in this country, the dance naturally took a patriotic air,”
he says.
“Too, the steps of ‘Liberty Parade’ are not too conspicuous; couples
won’t be hesitant to try them.”
The routine, composed by Mrs. Edna Rothard Passapee of New
Jersey, can be danced by a group or by couples.
Here are the steps of "Liberty Parade”:
Figure Partners walk four steps side by side, face each other and
salute, clicking heels. Step to opposite side, click heels.
Figure 2: Shoulder to shoulder, the couple walks in a circle (one)
to the rhythm of the music.
Figure 3: Take partner for four two-steps. Three steps forward,
face forward, swing leg forward and backward.
Figure 4: March away from partner, four steps, step to one side,
click heels, salute and repeat other side.
Meet partner, take partner for polka circle and repeat from Figure
1. If dancing in group, partners are changed after Figure 4.
“Plymouth Rock,” also sponsored by the Dancing Masters, reverses
the jitterburg trend, for its keynote is smoothness.
Danced to a fox trot, the routine combines smooth rhythm with steps
not too difficult for the average bollroom dancer.
It goes like this:
Dancers take positions and with the rhythm of the music point heel,
point toe and step, close step forward for the next three counts.
Clap hands. Then with outstretched arms rock left, then right for
counts 2, 3 and 4. Girl turns under partner’s arm for two counts. Face
each other and clap hands. Then take dancing position for eight
counts of polka. Then the set of steps is repeated.
