Propsolas for inflation in the United
States such as those contained in the
Thomas amendment have been accom

quarters for currency tinkering of a
different and more permanent type.
Once prices have been raised to a prede
termined level by inflation, there are
some who believe the country should
“stabilize” them at the new level by
monetary manipulation. In other words,
they advocate a “managed currency.”
What do its advocates mean by a
"managed currency,” and what is to be
said lor the merits of such proposals?
The two most widely discussed schemes
of this fort are the so-called “com
modity dollar” plan of Prof. Irving
Fisher of Yale, and proposals for central
bank control advocated in fomewhat
varying forms by Prof. Gustav Cas- j
sel of Sweden and the British econo

Would Vary Gold Worth.
Prof. Fisher's idea, briefly, is that
the gold dollar should cease to be a
constant weight of gold with fluctuating
purchasing power, and should become a
fluctuating weight of gold with a more
or less constant purchasing power.
There would be no actual coinage of
gold under the Fisher scheme. The
Government's gold supply would be kept
in gold bars, and the actual circulation,
then as now. would consist of represent
ative money, i.e.. bank notes and token
coins. All of the currency would be

level. If prices, as measured by some
generally accepted index, fell 1 per cent

purchasable with the dollars would be
decreased 1 per cent, and vice versa.
How would such a plan be likely to
work?
In the first place, it would introduce
a new and indeterminable fluctuating
element in the foreign exchanges. In j
Article VI of this series, in which the j

the par of exchange betfe-'' two gold

n^Lone would be much the more seri

Would Aid Speculators.
A still more serious defect in the
Fisher plan—assuming it to be adopted,
