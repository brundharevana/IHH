Albert Einstein was born at Ulm in
March, 1879, of Jewish parents. His
father was a business man whose affairs
compelled h’m to visit foreign countries
very frequently. The young Einstein
received his elementary education in
Munich. He displayed no special ability
in any of the subjects studied.
When sixteen he went to Zurich to
take his entrance examination at the
polytechnic school, but failed to pass.
Two years later he was again refused
admission to the Polytechnicum. But
in spite of his obvious “thickheadedness”
Einstein was in reality a precocious
mathematical genius. Unknown to his
teachers, on his own account and by his
own methods, he was making profound
researches into the science of numbers.
His School Troubles
After a great deal of trouble, Einstein
was permitted to go to Aarau, where he
was given an opportunity to enter a lo
cal cantonal school without the ordeal
of a new entrance examination. There
he obtained his first certificate and with
it he was finally admitted to the Zurich
Polytechnicum.

Professor Grossman explains this fact
by saying that Einstein was impressed
by the evidence of error in the very na
ture of the experiments that were made
around him. He felt that the whole of
physics was in a blind alley, and he
thought that it was useless to continue
on a path of knowledge which led no
where.
He therefore occupied himself with
subjects and aspects of physics not pur
sued in the classroom. This of course
aroused prejudice against him, for he
appeared to be not only slow, but also
conceited. He was even advised by one
of his instructors to enter commerce.
Einstein had a hard time in getting his
diploma, hut he got it.
After his graduation in 1900 Einstein
came face to face with the difficulty of
earning a livelihood. He eked out a
precarious living by tutoring. Finally
he secured employment as a clerk in one
of the Swiss patent offices at Berne.
Here for seven years he devoted all
his leisure to the study of theoretical
mathematics. These studies gained for
him a reputation of a man devoid of
common sense, inclined to phantastic
speculation and a dreamer.
But presently he sent in a paper to a
mathematical society at Zurich on “A
New Direction of Molecular Dimen
sions.” Only a few mathematicians were
able to follow the calculations submitted.
But he surely raised himself in the es
teem of these few scientists. For in the
intricate formulae worked out by Ein
stein there was the germ of his first
prognostication, which an expedition of
astronomers recently verified by a study
of a sun eclipse.
Among the few who took Einstein
seriously was the great French mathe
matician, Henri Poincare. Reluctantly
the University of Zurich conferred upon
him the degree of Doctor.
Einstein continued his studies and
finally won the acclaim of scientists like
Bacquerel, Madame Curie and others.
A Scientist’s Prophecy
During the period of comparative ob
scurity, when Einstein was not as yet
the world figure which he is today, the
renowned Poincare passed the following
judgment on Einstein:
“Einstein is one of the most original
minds with which I have been brought
in contact.
“Despite his youth and the misunder
standing of him that prevails, he has
already won a place of honor among the
few who are doing great work for
science. What we must all especially
admire in Einstein is the facility with
which he adapts himself to conceptions
wholly new and draws from them infer
ences at once startling and sound.
“He does not remain attached to the
classical and accepted principles. Con
fronted by any problem in physics he is
swift in catching every conceivable con
sequence or conclusion to which it may
lead.
“The result, if worked out at once in
his fruitful mind by a prophetic view
of novel phenomena, is capable of verifi
cation by experiment.
“1 would not be understood to mean
that all his prophetic conceptions will
sustain the test of experiment or be ver
ified in experience whenever such re
searches are made possible. As Einstein
seeks new paths in all directions it will
doubtless be found that some of his de
partures lead into blind alleys, but it
may be anticipated that one of the new
lanes he is opening up will turn out to
be the right one, and that is sufficient
for every practical purpose.
“This is precisely the way one must
go to work. The function of physical
mathematics is to state the problems.
Experiment will afford solution.”
Life has fully borne out the import of
this evaluation of Einstein’s genius.—
(Union Bulletin).
