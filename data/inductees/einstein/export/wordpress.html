DRAFT — NOT REVIEWED Albert Einstein was born 14 March 1879 in Ulm, Germany.[1][2][3] Albert Einstein died on April 18, 1955 at Princeton, New Jersey.[2][1][4][5]

DRAFT — NOT REVIEWED Citizenship

DRAFT — NOT REVIEWED The Declaration of Intention for Albert Einstein is from January 15, 1936.[6][7] A photograph of Albert Einstein is affixed to the Declaration of Intention.[8] His Petition for Naturalization has petition number 4009, assigned by the U.S. District Court for the District of New Jersey, Trenton.[8] In 1940, Albert Einstein was photographed receiving from Judge Phillip Forman his certificate of American citizenship.[9][10] The record of naturalization for Albert Einstein at the National Archives at Philadelphia includes a Certificate of Arrival, Declaration of Intention, Petition for Naturalization, and Oath of Allegiance.[8]

DRAFT — NOT REVIEWED Research for the Navy

DRAFT — NOT REVIEWED Albert Einstein was working as a consultant to the United States Government during World War II.[11] Albert Einstein was doing research for the Bureau of Ordnance, in his study at Princeton, N.J.[12] Nine letters were written by Albert Einstein to Lieutenant Stephen Brunauer of the Research and Development Division.[13] The letters relate to his research with the Research and Development Division of the Navy, specifically underwater explosions and a proposed electromagnetic torpedo detonator.[13] His letter of May 17, 1943 is in regards to Albert Einstein participating in the Research and Development Division of the Navy to research Navy problems.[14] His letter of June 18, 1943 discusses his idea of an electro-magnetic device that will automatically explode a torpedo as it passes underneath a ship.[15] In a letter of June 29, 1943, Albert Einstein says that he will not be able to come to Washington, DC for health reasons.[16] In a letter of July 30, 1943, Albert Einstein reveals that he has come up with a mathematical simplification to analyze the impact an underwater explosion would have on the hull of an armored ship.[17] In a letter of August 13, 1943, Albert Einstein mentions an offer from Dr. Vannever Bush to accept an appointment as a consultant to Division 8 of the National Research Committee.[18] Signed copies of a contract and naturalization papers were enclosed with that letter.[18] In a letter of August 22, 1943, Albert Einstein gives his opinion on the best localization for installation of the torpedo explosion.[19] In a letter of September 1, 1943, Albert Einstein discusses the need to carry out experiments, as he will not be able to progress any further in the problem through mathematical calculation.[20] His letter of January 4, 1944 discusses his idea to have a torpedo positioned parallel to the wall of a ship before it exploded.[21] When he worked the problem out, the idea of a torpedo positioned parallel to the wall of a ship proved impossible.[21] His letter of October 15, 1944 discusses quick compression in a metal container.[22]

DRAFT — NOT REVIEWED Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists

DRAFT — NOT REVIEWED Albert Einstein was Chairman of the Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists.[23][24] In a letter of October 11, 1947, Albert Einstein wrote on behalf of the Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists regarding questions, topics, and warnings concerning the use of nuclear or atomic resources as a means for diplomatic or military solutions.[23] In a letter of November 29, 1947, Albert Einstein writes as Chairman of the Emergency Committee of Atomic Scientists discussing the issue of international atomic energy control.[24] In a letter of November 29, 1947, Albert Einstein pushes for an effort in public education about atomic energy.[24] The letter includes the article Atomic War or Peace, authored by Albert Einstein.[24] In Atomic War or Peace, Albert Einstein discusses his opinion on the need of a supranational agreement for the regulation and use of nuclear weaponry.[24]
